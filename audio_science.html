The Physics of Audio Curation: Formats & Acoustics

This document explains why modern digital convenience often destroys audio quality, how historical listening spaces outperformed modern devices, and why uncompressed master files are vital for preservation.

1. How MP3 Files Aggressively "Crush" Sound

An MP3 file is a lossly compression format designed in the 1990s to solve a problem that no longer exists: tiny internet bandwidth and small hard drives. To shrink a file down to 10% of its original size, the MP3 encoder throws away massive amounts of real musical data using a trick called psychoacoustic modeling.

  • Frequency Truncation - The encoder assumes human ears cannot hear high frequencies well, so it completely cuts off the top frequency shelf (often anything above 16 kHz or 18 kHz).
  • Acoustic Masking - If two sounds happen at the same time and one is louder, the encoder completely erases the quieter sound from the digital bitstream, assuming your brain will ignore it.
  • Phase and Space Destruction - MP3 compression squashes the delicate timing differences between the left and right audio channels. This entirely destroys the "stereo image," making the music sound flat, narrow, and thin.

2. Why a 1930s Family Radio Outperformed Modern Devices

It sounds strange to say that a wireless set from nearly a century ago had better sound quality than a modern smartphone or Bluetooth speaker, but it comes down to basic physics, cabinet design, and raw acoustic movement:

  • Physical Cabinet Resonance - 1930s radios were housed in large, heavy, mathematically sculpted wooden cabinets. Wood acts as a natural acoustic amplifier, providing a rich, warm, and woody resonance that plastic modern smart speakers cannot replicate.
  • Massive Air Displacement - Old radios utilized large speakers with heavy magnets. To hear bass and midrange frequencies accurately, a speaker must physically move air inside a room. Modern micro-speakers are too tiny to move air effectively, resulting in a hollow sound.
  • Pure Analog Signal Chain - Early broadcasting and valve amplification possessed zero digital quantization errors, zero compression artifacts, and zero harsh brick-wall limiting. The signal was a continuous, smooth, natural electrical wave.

3. Why WAV Masters Are Best for Historical Curation

For your premium archive vaults, using uncompressed WAV (Waveform Audio File Format) masters is the only way to preserve true historical integrity:

  • Mathematical Exactness - A WAV file stores raw, linear PCM audio data. Every single electrical measurement captured by your studio gear or vinyl ripping chain is preserved bit-for-bit, exactly as it happened in the real world.
  • Immunity to Generation Loss - Every time you edit, filter, or re-save an MP3, it recompresses and destroys more audio data. A WAV file can be opened, processed through your Boiling Frog DSP scripts, and re-saved infinitely without losing a single byte of acoustic fidelity.
  • Full Spectrum Preservation - WAV files preserve the entire audio spectrum from 0 Hz up to 22,050 Hz (and higher). This leaves the high-frequency space fully open, allowing your 20 kHz Section 23 ultrasonic Morse watermark to live securely inside the data layer.
